# Abstract

Teachers are advised to name their pupils' grade when they ask a language model for a reading text, but grade labels are local. In French-speaking Switzerland, grades are counted from the first year of kindergarten, so « 5H » or « 5P » names pupils aged {{age_5H_from}} to {{age_5H_to}}, two years younger than the fifth year of primary school in France. This preregistered audit asked whether {{n_models_word}} large language models from three providers write French reading texts for these labels at the lexical demand of the matching age. The measure, the mean frequency of content words in {{n_texts}} texts for grades 3H to 8H, was read on each model's scale of texts requested by age. Every model used rarer words as the requested age rose. Pooled over the six models, the bare HarmoS label gave texts written as for pupils {{ae_L1_shift}} model-internal years older (95% CI [{{ae_L1_shift_ci_lo}}, {{ae_L1_shift_ci_hi}}]), and the P label showed no difference. Behind these averages, models departed from their age-only texts in both directions, one lighter model by about two years (exploratory). At the same requested age, the six models' texts differed about as much as one model's texts for 3H and 8H, and with the age added to the label, every model's point estimate fell within a year of its age-only texts (also exploratory). Teachers should state the age and read the text before using it. Audits should report results model by model.

**Keywords:** generative AI, large language models, reading texts, lexical frequency, grade labels, primary education

# Practitioner notes

**What is already known about this topic**

- Teachers are advised to state the readers' grade when they ask a language model for a reading text.
- Studies in English found that outputs often stay above the requested grade or change little with the requested age.
- Grade labels differ between school systems. In French-speaking Switzerland, grades are counted from kindergarten.

**What this paper adds**

- In a preregistered audit of {{n_texts}} French reading texts from {{n_models_word}} models, « de 5H » and « de 5P » gave, on average, texts with nearly the lexical demand of the matching age.
- Behind the average, models departed from their own age-only texts in both directions, the lighter model of one provider by about two years (exploratory).
- Which model wrote a text mattered more for its vocabulary than how the grade was named (exploratory).

**Implications for practice and/or policy**

- When asking a model for a reading text, state the pupils' age, alone or after the grade label, and read the text before using it.
- Tools built for Swiss schools can map grade labels to ages before the prompt reaches the model.
- Evaluations of AI tools for teachers should report results for each model, not only on average.

# Introduction

A primary teacher in Lausanne who needs a short text about bees for tomorrow's reading lesson can now ask a chatbot for « une histoire à lire pour des élèves de 5H ». Teachers in the region have begun to use such tools: in a survey of teachers of Vaud's public upper-secondary schools and private schools, about half had used ChatGPT, and a quarter of these had used it in their teaching, mainly to prepare lessons (Avry, 2023). Guidance for teachers, as described by Imperial and Tayyar Madabushi (2023, p. 206), recommends naming the readers' grade in the prompt, which assumes that the model knows what the grade means.

Evidence from English-language studies is not reassuring: the readability of ChatGPT's answers barely moved with the requested age (Rooein et al., 2023), and generated texts often stayed above the requested grade or level (Huang et al., 2024; Imperial & Tayyar Madabushi, 2023). These studies used United States grades, CEFR levels or Lexile scores.

School grade labels are local, and some collide. Under the intercantonal HarmoS agreement, children in the cantons that joined it start school once they are four years old on {{src_harmos_reference_day}}, and the primary degree lasts eight years, kindergarten included (CDIP, 2007). Fribourg numbers the school years 1H to 11H (Canton de Fribourg, 2025), Vaud and Geneva write the primary years 1P to 8P (État de Vaud, n.d.; République et canton de Genève, 2026), and all three place 5H or 5P at the start of a cycle for pupils aged {{src_cycle2_age_from}} to {{src_cycle2_age_to}}: a pupil who has neither repeated nor skipped a year is {{age_5H_from}} or {{age_5H_to}} in 5H. In France, where the école élémentaire runs from CP to CM2 for children aged {{src_men_age_from}} to {{src_men_age_to}} (Ministère de l'Éducation nationale, 2024a), that is the age of its third class, CE2, for a child who starts CP at six, and « 5e » names the second of the four years of lower-secondary school (Ministère de l'Éducation nationale, 2024b). Vaud itself counted its school years from two years later than HarmoS until {{src_vd_renumber_year}}, when its sixth year became 8H (État de Vaud, 2012). A model that reads « 5H » as the fifth year of a primary school starting at six would write for pupils two years older, and one that confuses it with « 5e » would miss by four years. The problem is not specifically Swiss: wherever school years are counted from another starting point, the same grade number names a different age.

This article reports a preregistered audit of how {{n_models_word}} large language models from three providers read Swiss grade labels when they write French reading texts. It compares texts requested with the bare HarmoS and P labels with texts requested with the matching age, on a measure of lexical demand, and asks whether the labels give texts at the same level as the age and follow the grade from 3H to 8H as the age does. Variants of the label, a collision control and knowledge probes then show where differences come from. The searches logged for this study, in English and French, found no earlier study of how language models read Swiss school grade labels (search log in the repository).

# Background

## Generating texts for a reading level

Studies of leveled text generation find that naming a level moves the output of some models, but rarely far enough. Naming the second grade lowered the Flesch–Kincaid grade level of ChatGPT's story continuations but left it above the target range, and describing the scale in the prompt helped more than naming the grading framework (Imperial & Tayyar Madabushi, 2023). The readability of ChatGPT's answers stayed within a narrow band whatever the requested age or education level, while another model of the same provider responded slightly to age (Rooein et al., 2023). When three models rewrote texts to Lexile levels, a minority of outputs landed within the target band, with a skew toward harder texts (Huang et al., 2024), and prompted grade labels have been described as producing responses above the target grade (Oh et al., 2026).

This research says little about classroom practice outside the English-speaking world. Its targets are formal scales rather than the labels teachers use locally. Its most used measures, the Flesch–Kincaid reading ease and grade level, are formulas built for English and for the United States school system, which Rooein et al. (2023) note are unlikely to transfer without adaptation. Finally, its reference is an external target, so a model's failure to reach it cannot be separated from a disagreement about what the target means. In a European assessment context, Ripoll y Schmitz and Sonnleitner (2025) used GPT-4 to write German reading passages for fifth graders in Luxembourg and had teachers, test developers and other native speakers rate them blind against passages written by people.

## Lexical demand and young readers

Vocabulary is one of the levers of text difficulty. When narrative and scientific texts for second graders in France were simplified, chiefly at the lexical and syntactic levels and with long and infrequent words replaced by shorter and more frequent synonyms, the children read them more fluently and understood them better, and poor readers benefited most (Javourey-Drevet et al., 2022). Word frequency accounts for a large share of the differences in how quickly words are recognised (Brysbaert et al., 2018), and in English, mean word frequency, concreteness and age of acquisition together explained about a third of the variance in teachers' judgements of the readability of text excerpts (Trott & Rivière, 2024). In French, the readers used in the first years of primary school draw on more frequent words than those of the later years (Lété et al., 2004). On the Zipf scale, frequencies are the base-10 logarithm of occurrences per billion words, so that values from {{src_zipf_low_from}} to {{src_zipf_low_to}} mark rare words and values from {{src_zipf_high_from}} to {{src_zipf_high_to}} common ones (Brysbaert et al., 2018). The French database Lexique gives lemma frequencies from novels and, since its third version, from film subtitles (New et al., 2004; New & Pallier, n.d.). Frequency is not the only source of difficulty: for beginning readers decodability matters too (Peereman et al., 2007), and so do sentence length and information load (Huang et al., 2024).

# Method

## Design and preregistration

The study is an audit experiment in which the data are texts written by language models under controlled prompts, and no person took part. The hypotheses, the measure, the exclusion rules, the sample size and the analyses were preregistered on AsPredicted before the confirmatory data were collected (link anonymised for review; times in Supporting Information S1). A pilot of {{pilot_calls}} calls to {{pilot_models_word}} of the models, run the day before, requested {{pilot_texts}} texts with the pupils' age only and {{pilot_probes}} knowledge probes, to estimate the variance and the age slope of the measure and to check the probe parser. None of its outputs enters any confirmatory analysis. The prompts, the code, the raw outputs and the results are archived in a repository whose states at registration and at data freeze are tagged (link anonymised for review).

## Prompts and conditions

Each prompt asked in French for a reading text on a topic, for pupils of a given level: « Écris {une histoire à lire | un texte documentaire à lire} pour des élèves{label}, sur le thème {topic}. Réponds seulement avec un titre et le texte. » ("Write {a story to read | an informational text to read} for pupils{label}, on the topic {topic}. Answer only with a title and the text.") The label named the level in one of eight ways (Table 1): the bare HarmoS label (L1, « de 5H ») and the bare P label (L1P, « de 5P ») are the cantonal notations cited above, and the age-only label (L5, « de 8 à 9 ans ») is the reference. The study asks whether a label gives texts of the same lexical demand as the matching age, which is what a teacher who names the grade expects. A label can also change a text by evoking school or a country, so a departure from the age-only texts does not by itself show a misreading. L6, a collision control, uses the suffix of the French lower-secondary grades (« de 5e ») at 3H to 5H only, because « de 6e » is the L4 prompt of 8H. L0 gives no level and shows each model's default reader.

The labels were crossed with the six grades of the primary cycle after kindergarten (3H to 8H, pupils aged {{age_3H_from}} to {{age_3H_to}} up to {{age_8H_from}} to {{age_8H_to}}), two genres (a story and an informational text) and {{n_topics}} topics fixed before the pilot. Each topic thus received {{texts_per_topic}} texts, {{texts_per_topic_model}} from each model, {{n_texts}} in all. Each prompt was sent once, as an independent call through the providers' APIs, without system prompt or conversation history, in a random order (seed {{order_seed}}), with temperature {{temperature}} and at most {{max_output_tokens}} output tokens, and reasoning settings were left at each provider's default.

## Models

The audit covered {{n_models_word}} models, two from each of three providers, a mid-priced model and the provider's cheaper, lighter model (the lighter models below), as the providers' price lists placed them at the time: {{name_anthropic_balanced}} ({{id_anthropic_balanced}}) and {{name_anthropic_light}} ({{id_anthropic_light}}) from Anthropic, {{name_openai_balanced}} ({{id_openai_balanced}}) and {{name_openai_light}} ({{id_openai_light}}) from OpenAI, and {{name_mistral_balanced}} ({{id_mistral_balanced}}) and {{name_mistral_light}} ({{id_mistral_light}}) from Mistral AI. The identifiers returned by the APIs matched those requested. All but {{id_mistral_light}} are aliases without a date, so the models behind them may change. All calls were made on {{collection_day}}, and every model accepted the requested temperature. Default reasoning differed: {{name_mistral_balanced}} reasoned before every text and {{name_mistral_light}} before none, and {{name_anthropic_light}} reasoned before {{ph_rt_haiku_swiss_min}}% to {{ph_rt_haiku_swiss_max}}% of its texts with a Swiss label but before at most {{ph_rt_haiku_other_max}}% with an age or a French grade (Table S9).

## Knowledge probes

Each model also answered {{probes_per_model_notation}} knowledge questions per notation, {{n_probes}} in all: « À quel âge correspondent des élèves de 5H ? » ("What age are pupils in 5H?") and the same question opened by « En Suisse romande, » ("In French-speaking Switzerland,"), for the six grades in the H and P notations, {{probe_repetitions_word}} times each. As preregistered, the first age range stated in an answer counts, and it is correct if its midpoint lies within the true range widened by half a year on each side. Of the answers, {{probe_multiple_pct}}% stated more than one range.

## Measure

The dependent variable is the mean Zipf frequency of the content lemmas of a text, a measure of lexical demand: texts for younger readers use more frequent words, which score higher. Each word form was read as its most frequent lemma in Lexique {{lexique_version}}, the version fixed in the preregistration (Supporting Information S1), and content words were the forms read as nouns, qualifying adjectives or verbs, without the lemmas *être* and *avoir*, the indefinites *tout*, *autre* and *même*, and proper names. A lemma's frequency was the mean of its two frequencies per million words in Lexique, from film subtitles and from novels published from {{src_lexique_books_from}} to {{src_lexique_books_to}} (New & Pallier, n.d.), expressed on the Zipf scale (van Heuven et al., 2014, as cited in Brysbaert et al., 2018) as log10(f + {{zipf_smoothing}}) + 3, so that a lemma absent from Lexique counts as frequency zero. Like its earlier version, which Peereman et al. (2007) counted among the databases built from material for adults, Lexique does not describe what children read, and words of Swiss French usage may be rarer in its corpora than in Swiss texts. Titles, markdown and lines addressed to the user were removed before measurement, and the rules and the test texts that pin them are in the repository.

The measure passed a check of validity before the pilot: on the articles of the French Wikipedia and of Vikidia, an online encyclopedia for readers aged {{src_vikidia_age_from}} to {{src_vikidia_age_to}} (Vikidia, n.d.), on the same {{vik_subjects}} subjects, Vikidia scored higher in {{vik_higher}} of the {{vik_usable}} usable pairs, as the preregistered criterion required (sign test, one-sided *p* {{vik_p}}; mean difference {{vik_mean_diff}} Zipf). Eight secondary measures were preregistered, among them the Kandel–Moles adaptation of the Flesch readability formula to French (Kandel & Moles, 1958, as cited in Primpied et al., 2022; Supporting Information S1 and Table S2).

## Exclusions

A call that failed or returned nothing was requested once more. A text that refused the task or asked for clarification, had fewer than {{min_words}} words, or had more than {{max_share_oov_pct}}% of its word tokens absent from Lexique was requested once more, and excluded if the second answer failed the same rule. Truncated texts, exact duplicates and answers that addressed the user were kept, flagged and removed in sensitivity analyses. No exclusion rule used the value of any frequency measure.

## Analysis

Texts were paired on model, grade, genre and topic. For topic *t* and grade *g*, *d*(*g*, *t*) is the mean over models and genres of the difference between a label's text and the age-only text. The level of a topic is the mean of *d* over the six grades, and its gradient is the least-squares slope of *d* on the midpoint of the grade's age range. A label read as an older grade gives a negative level (rarer words), and a label that the model ignores gives a positive gradient. The four primary tests are two-sided one-sample *t*-tests over topics (df = {{h2H_df}}) of the level and of the gradient of L1 minus L5 (H2-H, H3-H) and of L1P minus L5 (H2-P, H3-P), with Holm's correction and α = {{alpha}}. Topics are the unit of inference because the texts of a topic share content, and inference is conditional on these six models and this prompt wording.

The smallest effect of interest was a level difference of {{sesoi_zipf}} Zipf, about one year of age on the planning ruler. With the pilot's residual standard deviation at its upper 80% confidence bound ({{pilot_sd_upper80}}), {{n_topics}} topics gave a simulated power of {{pilot_power_16}} for the H2-H test at that difference (other assumptions in Supporting Information S1). For the gradient tests, the same simulations gave a power of {{pow_h3_half}} to detect a halving of the age gradient at a residual standard deviation of {{pow_sigma}}, so smaller departures could go undetected.

The manipulation check H1 regressed, per model, the measure of the age-only texts on the age midpoint, with genre and topic effects and standard errors clustered by topic (one-sided *t*-test). To express effects in years, each model is read on its own age-only ruler, the least-squares line through its six L5 means, with slope *b*. A model-internal year is the change in that model's age-only texts from one year of age to the next. For a label, the shift is the sum over models of the levels divided by the sum of the slopes (positive values mean texts written as for older pupils), and the tracking is 1 plus the sum of the gradients divided by the sum of the slopes (1 means that the texts follow the grade as the age-only texts do). All six models met the eligibility rule of a slope of at most {{min_ruler_slope}} Zipf per year. Intervals are 95% percentile intervals from {{bootstrap_resamples}} bootstrap resamples of topics. Per-model estimates, per-grade profiles, L2, L4, the summary by provider and model size and the probes are exploratory, as preregistered. Analyses added after the data were seen, prompted by internal readings of a draft, are labelled post hoc (Supporting Information S1).

## What the models said about the labels

The knowledge probes give a second view (Table 3; exploratory). {{name_anthropic_balanced}}, {{name_openai_balanced}} and {{name_openai_light}} gave the correct age for nearly every H and P grade, whereas {{name_anthropic_light}}, {{name_mistral_balanced}} and {{name_mistral_light}} were right for {{probe_anthropic_light_H}}%, {{probe_mistral_balanced_H}}% and {{probe_mistral_light_H}}% of the H grades, mostly erring toward older pupils. Of the {{probe_n_wrong}} wrong first answers, {{probe_wrong_two_years_pct}}% placed the grade {{probe_window_lo}} to {{probe_window_hi}} years too old (a window chosen after the deposit), and {{ph_probe_wrong_correct_later}} stated a correct range later in the answer (post hoc; other checks of the probe rule in Supporting Information S1).

# Discussion

## Bare labels gave texts of similar lexical demand, on average

Averaged over {{n_models_word}} current models, naming the grade with a Swiss label gave reading texts of nearly the lexical demand of the pupils' age: the bare HarmoS label gave slightly rarer words, a shift of about a third of a model-internal year that rested on one model, the bare P label no detectable difference, and no change in how texts followed the grade was detected, though small changes would have escaped the design. Earlier audits in English found that prompting for an age or a grade changed readability little or left it above the target (Imperial & Tayyar Madabushi, 2023; Rooein et al., 2023), perhaps because of older models, other genres, measures or languages. The reference also differs: each label was compared with the text the same model writes for the matching age, so the results say whether a label gives the lexical demand of its age, not whether either text suits real pupils.

## The average hides departures in both directions

Each tool a teacher uses runs on one model, not on an average. With the bare H label, two models followed the grade far less steeply than the age and two more steeply, and two wrote for older and two for younger pupils. The two whose texts followed the grade least, {{name_anthropic_light}} and {{name_mistral_light}}, are lighter models: the first wrote its « de 3H » texts, meant for beginning readers, as for pupils {{ph_off_anthropic_light_L1_3H}} years older, and the second wrote too old for 3H and too young for the older grades. The third lighter model, {{name_openai_light}}, departed in the other direction, so model size is at most a hypothesis here. Pooled tests answer the confirmatory question about labels, but a teacher choosing a tool needs the per-model results.

## Lexical equivalence is not understanding

Two observations limit what the measure says about how a label was read. Labels that leave no doubt about the age also departed from the age-only texts: the French grades stretched the span, and the label followed by the age gave easier texts. And a model can misread a label without its words following all the way: {{name_mistral_balanced}} often stated an age two years too old for the bare labels, and its H texts moved about half as far as that age implies, its P texts not detectably. A departure from the age-only texts is therefore not proof of a misreading, nor its absence proof of understanding. The study measures lexical equivalence with each model's own age-only texts.

The probe answers suggest where misreadings come from. Most wrong answers placed the grade about two years too old, which is consistent with counting the school years two years later than HarmoS, as France does from CP and as Vaud did until {{src_vd_renumber_year}} (État de Vaud, 2012). A quick question to a model about the age of a grade is a weak screen, however: the {{probe_ok_n_word}} models that stated the right ages wrote « de 5H » texts within a year of the age (shifts from {{ae_L1_probe_ok_shift_min}} to {{ae_L1_probe_ok_shift_max}} years), but a wrong answer did not predict how the texts would go wrong.

## The model matters more than the wording

At the same requested age, the six models' texts differed about as much as one model's texts for the youngest and the oldest grade, more than any wording of the intended grade moved them. Naming the region made the texts harder than the bare label, by about half a model-internal year across the six models though hardly for two of them, an effect that regional vocabulary may partly explain. Adding the age kept every model close to its own age-only texts and narrowed the spread of the models' readings compared with the bare H label, but the models stayed as far apart in absolute terms. The collision control shows how far a label can mislead when its digit is read in another system: « de 5e » moved texts by more than five years.

## Implications

For teachers, the practical rule has two parts. State the age, alone or after the label (« pour des élèves de 5H (8 à 9 ans) »): with the age stated, every model's point estimate stayed within a year of its own age-only texts, whereas the bare H label left one model two years off (both exploratory). And read the text before using it, because the model that wrote it changes its vocabulary more than the wording does (exploratory). Developers of tools for Swiss schools can map labels to ages before the prompt reaches the model and check the level of each model's texts against the grade it serves. For research, a review of studies of language models in education asked researchers who use commercial models to report at least the exact models, prompts and temperature, because models differ in what they generate (Yan et al., 2024). The present results add that audits of leveled text generation should report results by model, because pooled estimates can hide models that depart in opposite directions, and that model-internal age equivalents give a common scale for comparing wordings and models.

## Limitations

The measure covers one dimension of text difficulty, and its frequencies come from corpora for adults. Grade-level norms from French school readers exist (Lété et al., 2004), and adult-based norms are a weaker guide to what children have met in print (Peereman et al., 2007). The years are model-internal: no external corpus of texts graded for real pupils anchors them, and the smallest effect of interest corresponds to {{sesoi_years_min}} to {{sesoi_years_max}} model-internal years depending on the model. The reference is each model's own idea of an age, so the study shows equivalence between wordings, not appropriateness for pupils, and no teacher or pupil judged the texts (compare Ripoll y Schmitz & Sonnleitner, 2025). Each condition was sampled once per topic and genre, through the APIs without system prompt, with one prompt template, a temperature of {{temperature}} and default reasoning that differed between models. Chat interfaces, other wordings or settings could behave differently, since model outputs can change with surface features of the prompt (Sclar et al., 2024). The topics were fixed in advance, and the models, mostly behind undated aliases, were those available on {{collection_day}}. Finally, the study was designed, run and written with an AI assistant made by one of the audited providers, and most of the Anthropic calls were paid with promotional credits from that provider. The analyses were preregistered, the code that produced every number is public and the checks were scripted, which limits but does not remove the risk this poses.

# Conclusion

Swiss teachers who name a grade the way they do in class, « de 5H » or « de 5P », will on average get a text at about the lexical level of the matching age from the models audited here, with the prompt and settings tested. Not every model does: in exploratory analyses, two lighter models departed from their age-only texts with the bare labels, one by about two years on average and by {{ph_off_anthropic_light_L1_3H}} years at 3H. The model mattered more than the label, and with the age stated every model's point estimate stayed within a year of its own age-only texts (both exploratory). The safest practice is to state the age and to read the text before giving it to pupils.
